Testing a Hypothesis, Step by Step

One question, one sample: what it means to use a sample to challenge H0, then three ways to make the decision: confidence interval, critical value, and p-value.

The business question

Hammermill cuts paper for laser printers. The target width is 216 mm. Paper that is too wide or too narrow jams printers. Quality Control wants to know: is the machine still cutting at 216 mm on average, or has it drifted?

Target μ₀
216 mm
Known σ
0.023 mm
Sample size n
50 sheets
Significance α
.05

σ is known from years of production data. We will use the sample in a moment — first we set up the test.

Write H₀ and H₁

Null hypothesis · status quoH₀: μ = 216

The machine is on target.

Alternative · what we look forH₁: μ ≠ 216

The machine has drifted, in either direction. "≠" means a two-tailed test.

μ is the true average width of all paper the machine cuts. We cannot measure all of it, so we never know μ for sure. We will use a sample of 50 sheets to decide whether "μ = 216" is still believable.

The key move: assume H₀ is true

A test starts by assuming H₀ is correct: the true mean really is μ = 216. Then we ask: "If that were true, what kind of sample means would we expect to see?"

From the Central Limit Theorem (Week 8), the sample mean x̄ of n = 50 sheets is a random variable with a normal distribution, centered at the true mean:

If H₀ is true:   x̄ ~ N( μ₀ = 216 ,   σ/√n = 0.023/√50 = 0.003253 )

The curve below is the world of H₀: every place a sample mean could land, and how likely it is, if the machine is truly on target.

Distribution of x̄ if H₀ is true

The standard error 0.003253 tells you the typical distance between a sample mean and 216, just from random sampling.

Think first: where is x̄ likely to land?

If H₀ is true, would a sample mean like each of these be likely or unlikely? Decide for each, then check.

Your guesses on the H₀ curve

Check with a simulation

Pretend the machine is truly on target (H₀ true) and draw 100 samples of 50 sheets. Each dot is one sample mean.

Most sample means land within about 2 standard errors of 216. Values far out in the tails can happen when H₀ is true, but rarely — only about 5 in 100 land more than 0.0064 (1.96 standard errors) away from 216. Those are the red dots.

Choose a method to finish the test

Steps 1–4 are the same for every method: we assumed H₀ is true and saw where sample means should land. Now there are three ways to make the decision. All three use the same α = .05, so they always reach the same decision. Pick one, and come back to try the others.

Confidence interval method

Where is "too far" from 216?

We need a rule before looking at our sample. With α = .05, we call the middle 95% of the H₀ curve "believable" and the outer 5% (2.5% in each tail) "too surprising if H₀ were true".

216 ± 1.96 × 0.003253 = 216 ± 0.006375 = [ 215.9936 , 216.0064 ]

1.96 is the same z you used for a 95% confidence interval in Week 8. In Excel: =NORM.S.INV(0.975). The amount 0.006375 is the margin of error.

The believable zone if H₀ is true (α = .05)
If our x̄ lands inside the green zone → nothing surprising → no reason to doubt H₀.
If our x̄ lands in a red tail → that would be rare if H₀ were true → evidence against H₀.

The same check, using the confidence interval

In practice we build the 95% confidence interval around x̄ (Week 8), and check whether 216 is inside it:

x̄ ± 1.96 × σ/√n = 216.007 ± 0.006375 = [ 216.0006 , 216.0134 ]

Confidence interval method

Make the decision and say what it means

Decision: reject H₀ at α = .05.
216 is outside the 95% CI [216.0006, 216.0134].
Conclusion for the manager: "The sample provides evidence that the average paper width is different from 216 mm. The whole interval is above 216, so the machine is cutting slightly too wide. Quality Control should recalibrate the cutting tools."

Note: rejecting H₀ does not mean we are 100% sure. There is still a 5% risk that the machine was fine and we were unlucky (a Type I error). That risk is α.

The recipe (two-tailed test with a CI)

  1. Write H₀: μ = μ₀ and H₁: μ ≠ μ₀.
  2. Assume H₀ is true. Then x̄ ~ N(μ₀, σ/√n).
  3. Take the sample and compute x̄.
  4. Build the CI: x̄ ± z × σ/√n, with z = 1.96 for α = .05 (=NORM.S.INV(1-α/2)).
  5. μ₀ inside the CI → fail to reject H₀.   μ₀ outside the CI → reject H₀.
  6. Write the conclusion in business language.

"Fail to reject" is not "prove": if 216 were inside the CI, we would say the sample gives no strong evidence of drift, not that the machine is exactly on target.

Critical value method

Switch to the z scale

Instead of working in millimeters, measure how many standard errors x̄ is from 216:

z = (x̄ − μ₀) / (σ/√n) = (x̄ − 216) / 0.003253

If H₀ is true, z follows the standard normal distribution N(0, 1): centered at 0, with standard deviation 1. It is the same curve as Step 3; only the axis changed. The gray labels under the axis show the matching x̄ in millimeters.

Distribution of z if H₀ is true
Critical value method

Draw the rejection regions (α = .05)

Before looking at the sample, mark where z would be too surprising if H₀ were true. The test is two-tailed, so α = .05 is split: 2.5% in each tail.

Critical values = ± zα/2 = ± 1.96    Excel: =NORM.S.INV(1-0.05/2)
Rejection regions for α = .05
Rule: reject H₀ if zcalc < −1.96 or zcalc > +1.96. Otherwise, fail to reject H₀.
This is the same 1.96 used for the 95% confidence interval.
Critical value method

Take the sample and compute zcalc

Quality Control measures 50 sheets: x̄ = 216.007 mm. Standardize it:

zcalc = (216.007 − 216) / 0.003253 = 2.152

Our sample mean is 2.152 standard errors above 216. Before you click: where does 2.152 fall?

Our zcalc on the H₀ curve
2.152 > 1.96, so zcalc lands in the right rejection region. If the machine were truly cutting at 216, a sample mean this many standard errors away would be rare.

The recipe (two-tailed test with critical values)

  1. Write H₀: μ = μ₀ and H₁: μ ≠ μ₀.
  2. Assume H₀ is true. Then z = (x̄ − μ₀)/(σ/√n) follows N(0, 1).
  3. Find the critical values ± zα/2 (=NORM.S.INV(1-α/2); 1.96 for α = .05).
  4. Take the sample and compute zcalc.
  5. |zcalc| > zα/2 → reject H₀.   Otherwise → fail to reject H₀.
  6. Write the conclusion in business language.

p-value method

How surprising is our sample?

Quality Control measures 50 sheets: x̄ = 216.007 mm. As a z-score:

zcalc = (216.007 − 216) / 0.003253 = 2.152
The p-value answers one question: If H₀ is true, what is the probability of getting a sample mean at least this far from 216, in either direction?

Before you compute: do you expect that probability to be large or small?

Small. In Step 4, sample means more than about 2 standard errors from 216 were rare when H₀ was true, and ours is 2.152 standard errors away.
p-value method

The p-value is the area beyond ±2.152

H₁ is μ ≠ 216, so being too far above or too far below both count as extreme. We add the area in both tails.

Area at least as extreme as our sample, if H₀ is true
one tail = 1 − NORM.S.DIST(2.152, TRUE) = .0157   →   p-value = 2 × .0157 = .0314

In one Excel step: =2*(1-NORM.S.DIST(ABS(z),TRUE)). NORM.S.DIST gives the area to the left of z, so 1 minus it is the right tail.

p-value method

Compare the p-value with α = .05 and decide

Rule: reject H₀ if p-value < α. Otherwise, fail to reject H₀.
α = .05 is the bar we set before seeing the data; the p-value is what the data delivered.
p-value vs. α on the probability scale
Decision: reject H₀ at α = .05.
p-value = .0314 < .05. If the machine were on target, a sample this extreme would happen only about 3 times in 100.
Conclusion for the manager: "The sample provides evidence that the average paper width is different from 216 mm. The machine is cutting slightly too wide; Quality Control should recalibrate the cutting tools."

Same decision as the other two methods. The p-value adds one thing: how strong the evidence is (smaller p = stronger).

The recipe (two-tailed test with the p-value)

  1. Write H₀: μ = μ₀ and H₁: μ ≠ μ₀.
  2. Assume H₀ is true. Then z = (x̄ − μ₀)/(σ/√n) follows N(0, 1).
  3. Take the sample and compute zcalc.
  4. Two-tailed p-value: =2*(1-NORM.S.DIST(ABS(z),TRUE)).
  5. p-value < α → reject H₀.   p-value ≥ α → fail to reject H₀.
  6. Write the conclusion in business language.
